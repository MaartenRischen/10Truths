Ten ways to make someone feel it in a minute

The brief we agreed, then ten one-paragraph films written against it. Rate them, tick the one or two you want, and leave notes; I read it all back. Nothing gets drawn or rendered until you've picked.

Job
The long film explains the idea. This one makes someone feel it in about a minute: they recognise themselves, feel relief that it isn't their fault, and click through to the long film or the site.
Audience
Someone scrolling on a phone who has never heard the word "mismatch".
Format
60 to 90 seconds, vertical first, with a horizontal cut. Works with the sound off.
Words
Close to none. The story is told in pictures and music and ends on one line plus the URL.
Shape
One character and one turn.

My shortlist

If I had to choose today. The other seven are real contenders, each for a different reason, and your pick beats mine.

01

The Visitor

60–90 svertical firston my shortlist

One night your ancestor from 50,000 years ago knocks on your door. He loves everything in your flat except one thing.

The film

Late at night a manikin eats noodles alone on the sofa, phone in hand. A knock. At the door stands a manikin identical to it: same wood, same joints, dust on its feet. The same model, fifty thousand years older. The visitor walks in and is delighted by everything. Water comes out of the wall. A switch turns night into day. The fridge is a cave full of food. Music comes out of a little box. He laughs at all of it. Then he starts looking for the others. He checks the bedroom, the balcony and the stairwell, and comes back confused. He looks at his host, then at the silent flat, and does the only thing he knows: he sits down close, shoulder to shoulder. The host looks at the phone, types one message and sends it. Last shot: the flat full of people, the visitor in the middle, laughing again.

Why it works
His delight at running water and light switches makes the film pro-technology from the first minute, so the one thing he can't find hits harder. Two identical manikins say "everything changed, except you" without a word.
Risk
One message filling the flat can feel too easy. The ending has to feel earned.
Made with
Blender: two manikins, one flat. Or gen-video from locked references.

02

Care Instructions

60 svertical firston my shortlist

A wooden manikin comes in a box with a care card nobody reads, and slowly stops standing up.

The film

An office desk. Someone unboxes a painter's manikin, poses it upright next to the monitor and throws the box under the desk. Days pass in quick cuts from the same angle, and the manikin slumps a little more each day. They try to fix it: tighten its joints, oil the wood, give it a better stand, stick a motivational sticker on its chest, buy a shinier one to stand beside it. The new one starts slumping too. Clearing out the desk, someone finds the card at the bottom of the box: "Care instructions. Keep with its people, never alone. Needs daylight, and darkness at night. Hold often. Give it work that others need. Let it walk every day." Last shot: a sunny windowsill, a small crowd of manikins arranged together, busy with something, all standing.

Why it works
The whole thesis as an object story: we keep repairing the individual when the conditions are wrong. The card is Cor in one prop, and real painter's manikins do come in boxes.
Risk
The repair montage has to stay gentle and funny: a joke about self-improvement culture, never about therapy or medicine.
Made with
Blender, in the miniature look from the treatment. Or a real stop-motion shoot on a desk with real manikins, which would cost almost nothing.

03

The Night Watch

60–75 svertical firston my shortlist

Someone checks the locks again at 3 a.m. Then we see the job their body thinks it's doing.

The film

3:07 a.m. A manikin lies awake, gets up, checks the door lock, checks the window, scrolls the news, checks the lock again. It looks like anxiety, and it is. Cut, on the same pose, to the same manikin sitting on a rock at the edge of a sleeping camp, scanning the dark. It was a job. Behind it the band sleeps, because someone is awake. Before dawn another figure gets up, touches its shoulder and takes over the watch. It lies down in the warm pile of bodies and is asleep in seconds. Back in the flat it's 3:09, and nobody is coming to take over. Then the phone lights up with a message from the neighbour across the hall: "Up with the baby again. I'm awake if you need anything." The manikin puts the phone down and sleeps.

Why it works
The viewer judges the character as anxious, then has to take it back. A study of Hadza sleep found almost no moment in the night when the whole camp was asleep at once. The fix at the end is modern and small: a neighbour.
Risk
The watch has to read instantly without words. The neighbour's message is the only text.
Made with
Blender or gen-video, with the flat and the camp matched shot for shot.

04

Good Dog

60 svertical first

Everyone knows why a dog left alone all day chews the sofa. Nobody applies that logic to themselves.

The film

A wooden dog, the kind artists use as a model, alone in a flat for ten hours: pacing, whining at the door, tearing a cushion apart. The owner comes home to the mess, and the viewer already knows exactly what the dog needed: a walk, company, something to do. Then the owner drops onto the sofa and does the same things. Opens the fridge, closes it, opens it again. Paces. Scrolls. Can't settle. The dog watches the owner doing everything it did all day, walks over and puts its head on the owner's knee. Last shot: both of them outside in a courtyard full of neighbours and dogs. The dog is running. The owner is talking to someone.

Why it works
People already accept the environment explanation for animals. The film borrows that instinct and turns it on the viewer in one cut. It also connects to the animal series and The Enclosure.
Risk
The dog can steal the film. The owner's half needs to be as specific as the dog's.
Made with
Blender (a wooden dog and a manikin) or gen-video.

05

The Grid and the Circle

60 svertical first

One continuous shot up the face of an apartment block: fifteen people alone in fifteen windows. Then the building opens.

The film

A slow crane up a night facade, made for a vertical screen. Fifteen lit windows, one manikin in each, each in the middle of a mismatch: scrolling in bed, eating at a desk, running on a treadmill that faces a wall, saying goodnight to a face on a screen, a child with a tablet. Then the walls between the flats fold down, the floors tilt, and every figure slides gently into one shared courtyard at the heart of the building. The fifteen cold screen glows drain into one warm light at the centre, and the same fifteen gestures finish there: the scroller watches the fire pit, the runner arrives somewhere, the goodnight becomes a hand on a real shoulder, the child wrestles with other kids.

Why it works
One unbroken move and one visual rule. The poster (the grid beside the circle) comes free, and every loop visibly closes. Changed from the August version: the circle forms in a modern courtyard in the same building, instead of around a campfire in the wild, so nobody can read it as "go back".
Risk
Fifteen characters leaves no single one to love. It's more spectacle than heartbreak.
Made with
Blender: one rig, fifteen instances.

06

The Human Zoo

60–75 svertical first

A zoo enclosure for our own species, and a keeper who finally reads the care standard.

The film

A glass enclosure in a zoo. Inside is a small modern flat and one manikin: pacing the same short track in front of a screen, eating from a bag without looking, rubbing its own arm over and over. Visitors, also manikins, stop and look in, sad. The placard reads: "Homo sapiens. Behaviour: stereotypic. Cause: enclosure." A keeper opens a thick binder of the species' requirements and starts rebuilding. A wall comes out so the next enclosure joins this one. Then a window, a shared table, a garden, other humans. The pacing stops. Outside the glass the visitors look at each other, then down at their own phones.

Why it works
The sharpest and most shareable of the ten, and the joke is true: EU law requires zoos to provide species-specific enrichment. One placard carries the thesis.
Risk
Satire can look like contempt for the one behind the glass, so the keeper's care has to carry the warmth. The claim needs a legal check before release ("no general standard for humans", never "no law").
Made with
Blender or gen-video.

07

Wrong Size

60–90 svertical first

A 30 cm manikin lives in a full-size world where everything was built for someone else's scale.

The film

Real-scale sets, shot from the manikin's height. The door handle is out of reach. The phone is the size of a mattress. The feed is a lit wall scrolling past like a train. The city through the window never ends. It climbs, pushes and keeps up. At night it sits on the edge of a huge table, small. Then it notices an architect's model on a shelf: a neighbourhood built at its size. It climbs in, and everything fits. Doors it can open, a square where paths cross, a table with others, daylight, a little tram. Tiny in the real world, exactly right in this one.

Why it works
"Human-sized" becomes a physical feeling, and the answer at the end is a designed, modern place, which is exactly what Demismatch argues for.
Risk
It's close to direction B from the treatment, and it has to stay a character story rather than a tour of sets.
Made with
Blender with direction B's pipeline, or a real miniature shoot.

08

Two Bars

60 svertical first

The needs bars from The Sims, except every need has two bars, and only one of them ever fills.

The film

A game-style overhead view of a manikin's flat. Each need (belonging, rest, purpose, touch) has two bars: a bright one for how it feels and a dim one for whether it's met. Every action fills a bright bar fast, with a satisfying chime: a like, a snack, a notification, one more episode. The dim bars never move, and the bright ones drain back within seconds. The manikin speeds up, doing more of everything, and the chimes come faster until it's frantic. Then the cursor clicks the front door. Outside: a neighbour, a shared meal, a walk, a hand on the shoulder. The dim bars fill slowly and stay full. The chimes stop, and you hear the room.

Why it works
Everyone already reads this game language. It explains the difference between a proxy and a real need in seconds, and it can become a playable browser game later.
Risk
The cleverest of the ten and the least tender. The quiet ending has to carry the heart.
Made with
Code, with the WebGL and 2D pipelines. The fastest of the ten to make.

09

Mara's Awake

60 svertical first

At 2 a.m. someone tells an AI they're lonely. The AI does the one thing a product built for engagement never would.

The film

2:04 a.m., a kitchen, a manikin typing into a chat app. We see only the phone: "cant sleep. everyone's asleep and i feel like the only person awake in the world". The assistant answers kindly, asks a question and listens for a few messages. Then: "You mentioned Mara earlier. She's online right now. Maybe call her instead of talking to me?" The manikin stares at the screen, then presses call. Cut to another kitchen: Mara, another manikin, picks up. We never hear the words. We see the first manikin's shoulders drop, and the chat window sits unread in the background.

Why it works
The AI half of the message, shown instead of argued: technology that closes a loop instead of holding it open. It's topical, and it shows in one scene what "aligned to human needs" means.
Risk
It needs a few lines of on-screen text, and an AI story can be misread as an ad for AI friends. The "call her" line was taken out of the main film in June.
Made with
Code and Blender: mostly a phone screen and two kitchens.

10

The Elders

60–90 svertical first

Old people describe the life they grew up in. The mismatch happened within one lifetime.

The film

Real faces, filmed upright for phones, somewhere near where you live. Each elder answers one question. Who was around you when you were small? Where did you sleep? Who did you eat with? How far did you walk in a day? What happened when someone was sad? Their answers are plain and specific, and they include the hard parts. Cut against the same places today: the same street, empty; the same house, a TV on. The last elder is asked what young people need now, and whatever they say ends the film.

Why it works
Real people and real memory. The most credible and most human of the ten, and nothing like any Demismatch film so far.
Risk
It needs a shoot (people, consent, translation, a small crew), so it's the one I can't make alone. It drifts into nostalgia unless the elders also talk about what's better now.
Made with
A camera and a translator on location; I do the edit, subtitles and grade.

The Enclosure, and what happens after you pick

The Enclosure stays the animal film. Good Dog and The Human Zoo are its closest relatives, so if you pick either, the two can run as a pair. The Visitor, Care Instructions and The Night Watch are the human stories it leaves out.

After you pick, I write the script and a board for your one or two, cut a rough animatic with temporary music, and we show it to a few people who have never heard of Demismatch before anything expensive gets rendered. Then we choose the look to fit the story: the miniature world, the threads of light, the ink line, a real stop-motion shoot on a desk, or something new.
